<h2 id="project">One project, many views</h2>

<aside className="article-note"><strong>A teaching case:</strong> Maple Campus is not a real organization. It is testing a better way for student clubs to book rooms. Its people, targets, requirements and risk ratings show how a business analyst’s assets fit together. You can use the same assets on projects in any industry. In this case, <strong>you are the business analyst</strong>.</aside>

Club organizers say that confirmations are slow and that they do not know which rooms are free. The project team will study the current process, agree on what is needed and test a small booking service with a few clubs.

The new service covers room requests, staff decisions, booking status and messages to requesters. It connects to the campus sign-in, calendar and email services that already exist. The pilot does not include payments, public event tickets or a new campus calendar.

<ExampleTable label="Facilitation plan agenda for a 90-minute discovery workshop">

| Time | Activity | How it works | What it produces |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0–10 min | Open the session | The sponsor explains the purpose. The facilitator reviews the agenda, roles and ground rules. | Everyone knows why the group is meeting |
| 10–30 min | Walk through how booking works today | Participants put the steps on a shared timeline. Organizers go first. Then Facilities add what happens after a request arrives. | A map of the current process |
| 30–50 min | Find the problems | Everyone writes problems on notes without talking. Then the group puts similar notes together and names each group. | Groups of related problems |
| 50–55 min | Break | — | — |
| 55–70 min | Prioritize | Each person gets a few dots and votes for the most important problems. Then the group discusses any surprising results. | The top three problems, ranked |
| 70–85 min | List what we do not know | Each person takes a turn to share an open question or assumption. Each one gets an owner and a date. | A list of open questions |
| 85–90 min | Close | The facilitator reviews the decisions and next steps. Each person gives one line of feedback on the session. | Agreed next steps |

</ExampleTable>

**After the session:** within two working days, you send a summary and ask participants to confirm or correct it. The results help you build the stakeholder list, the requirements and the cause-and-effect map below. The sponsor reviews the parking lot list.

These statements describe what the booking service must do and how to test it.

<h2 id="causes">Cause-and-effect mapping</h2>

Start with the problem on the left. Move right to the possible causes, and ask “Why?” at each step. Each arrow points from a possible cause to its effect. In this example, every arrow is a **hypothesis**: an idea to test, not a proven fact. Two notes, joined with dashed lines, suggest actions to test if the evidence supports those causes.

To test this map, look at when requests arrived and when decisions were made. Compare follow-up messages with the information in the first request. Ask approvers about backup cover and calendar updates. Look for other explanations before you call any branch a root cause.

<h2 id="risks">Short risk analysis</h2>

These example ratings are for the four-week pilot.

- **Likelihood** is how likely the risk is: 1 = unlikely, 2 = possible, 3 = likely.
- **Impact** is how much harm it causes: 1 = minor (small rework), 2 = significant (a real delay or disruption), 3 = serious (harm to the service, to access or to privacy).
- **Priority** is likelihood × impact.
- **Mitigation** is an action that reduces a risk. **Residual risk** is the risk that remains after mitigation. These ratings are estimates and still need review.

### Contingency plan for R01: the same room is confirmed twice

<div className="contingency">

1. **Trigger:** Facilities finds two confirmed bookings for the same room at overlapping times.
2. **First response:** Facilities stops new automatic confirmations for that room. IT Services saves the logs and moves the affected requests to manual review.
3. **Keep the service running:** One Facilities coordinator manages a controlled list of bookings. They solve the conflict using the agreed rules and offer another room or time. Club organizers tell attendees and confirm access arrangements.
4. **Escalate:** Tell the sponsor and the technical lead right away. If there is no workable option within two working hours, the sponsor makes the decision with Facilities and the affected organizers.
5. **Recover:** The technical lead fixes the problem and tests it again, including approvals at the same moment. Facilities checks the list against the calendar. Facilities restarts automatic confirmations only when no conflicts remain.

</div>
